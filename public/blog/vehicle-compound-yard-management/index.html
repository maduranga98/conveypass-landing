Vehicle Compound Management: How to Control Vehicles Inside Your Yard

Yard24 Nov 20263 min read

Most gate processes focus on the moment a vehicle enters. Vehicle compound management is about everything that happens after that, until the vehicle leaves.

Inside the compound, vehicles queue, manoeuvre, load, unload and sometimes stay far longer than planned. Without a process, the site loses track of who is inside and whether each visit actually did what it was meant to.

What is vehicle compound management?

Vehicle compound management is the control of vehicles between check-in and check-out within a site's yard, compound or loading area. It answers four questions at any moment:

  1. Which vehicles are on site right now?
  2. What is each vehicle here to do?
  3. Has that work been completed and checked?
  4. Has any vehicle been here too long?

1. Keep a live on-site list

The basic requirement is knowing who is inside. A paper register can tell you only if every exit was written down, and in practice exits are the entries most often missed.

A digital check-in and check-out creates a live list of vehicles on site. In an emergency evacuation, that list matters more than any other record you hold.

2. Stop double-booking drivers

A driver who is already checked in on one visit should not be able to start another. This happens more than expected with contractor fleets, where a driver might bring a second vehicle in before the first has checked out, or a pass is shared.

A simple rule closes the gap: a driver on an active visit cannot be checked in again until that visit is closed.

3. Verify unloading before check-out

The gate guard usually cannot see what happened at the loading bay. Assign a yard or loading-bay role to confirm unloading or loading before the vehicle is allowed to leave. Their confirmation should record:

  • Who confirmed it.
  • The time.
  • Any issue, such as a short delivery, damage or a refused load.

This creates a three-step visit, check-in, unloading check, check-out, with a named person responsible for each step.

4. Set a maximum dwell time

Vehicles that stay longer than expected can block bays, create safety risks and hide problems. Decide on a maximum time on site that fits your operations, and make overstays visible to supervisors.

The right limit varies. A site receiving bulk deliveries might expect visits to last an hour or two, while a site with overnight parking for contractor trucks might allow much longer. The important part is that the limit exists and is checked.

5. Handle incidents inside the compound

Speeding, unsafe reversing and other violations often happen inside the yard, not at the gate. Safety staff should be able to record a violation with photo evidence and apply a penalty to the driver, the vehicle or both. See managing gate incidents, penalties and suspensions.

6. Close every visit cleanly

Each visit should end with a check-out that records the time and the guard. Visits still open at the end of a shift should be investigated rather than ignored. They mean either a vehicle is still inside or the record is wrong.

Frequently asked questions

What is vehicle compound management?

It is the control of vehicles inside a site's yard or compound between check-in and check-out, including knowing who is on site, confirming unloading, enforcing time limits and recording incidents.

Why verify unloading before a vehicle checks out?

The gate guard usually cannot see the loading bay. A yard officer's confirmation proves the delivery or collection was completed and records any problems before the vehicle leaves.

What is a good maximum dwell time for vehicles on site?

It depends on your operations. Set a limit that fits normal visit lengths at your site and make overstays visible to supervisors, rather than using a single industry figure.

How do you stop a driver being checked in twice?

Use a rule that a driver on an active visit cannot be checked in again until that visit is closed. Digital gate systems can enforce this automatically.
